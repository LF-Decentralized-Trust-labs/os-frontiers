# Landscape reference: who is working on open source sustainability

> **Pass date:** 2026-10-27 PT
> **Stage 0 reference section.** No Hard Gate is passed. Nothing here is an audit, and nothing here changes `dospo/`, `omf/`, `orf/`, the whitepapers or the evaluator.

This section profiles the main organizations and programs paying for, routing, hosting or measuring open source maintenance in 2026. Each profile says what the organization does, what evidence exists for its scale, how it maps to the lab's three frameworks, and what the evidence does not show.

## How to read a profile

**OSF mapping.** Every profile names the framework layer it informs:

- **dOSPO** — who decides: governance, selection, transparency.
- **OMF** — how money goes out: retainers, grants, security work, payouts.
- **ORF** — how money comes back, using the families in [`orf/START_HERE.md`](../../orf/START_HERE.md): **A** structural network revenue, **B** enterprise earned revenue, **C** membership and certification, **D** voluntary contributions, **E** capital income.
- **Routing rail / allocation** — moves or splits money someone else gave. Under the ORF policy used elsewhere in this repo, a rail is **not replenishment**.

**Confidence**, as in the evidence register and survey inventory:

- **High** — a filing or program report states the figure, with method or page.
- **Sourced** — the cited page states it.
- **Medium** — the page states it, but it is one publisher's self-report, counter or press release.
- **Low** — secondary source, dated, or conflicting.
- **Unverified** — no page opened in this pass states it.

**Access**: *Fetched* (page opened on the pass date), *Search result* (seen in a search-result listing; page not opened), *Repo capture* (read from a capture already in this repo, with its path). No third-party pages or PDFs were added to the repository by this pass.

## What the landscape shows

These are judgments drawn from the profiles, not measurements.

1. **Almost all money goes one way.** Public funders, security funds, grants and corporate pledges pay out and rely on next year's budget. Only a few profiles show money that comes back from a source the body earns or holds: commercial support revenue partly returned to projects (HeroDevs, Tidelift, and the new Commonhaus program), membership dues (Linux Foundation, OpenSSF), and capital income (Open Source Endowment, still small).
2. **Rails are not sources.** GitHub Sponsors, thanks.dev, Ecosyste.ms Funds, Open Source Collective and Drips are good at splitting money. None of them create it. They are the natural payout layer for OMF programs.
3. **Security is where new money is going.** The largest 2026 commitments (the $12.5m to Alpha-Omega and OpenSSF, GitHub's $5.5m expansion, NSF's up to $40m) are framed around security and the rise in AI-generated vulnerability reports.
4. **Public funding is changing hands.** NLnet paused most calls between Commission cycles, the EU fund is still a pilot without a budget, and the US launched a new program. The German agency remains the steady reference point.
5. **Foundation retainers are arriving.** The Sovereign Tech Fellowship (14 people in 2026) and the Rust Foundation Maintainers Fund (launched June 2026) are the closest live matches to OMF's maintainer retainer.
