## 1. The Open Source Forking Dilemma

In open-source software (OSS), permissive licenses (e.g., Apache-2.0, MIT) grant any party the right to copy, modify, and redistribute code. Traditional monetization attempts relying on restrictive licensing (e.g., SSPL, Business Source License) frequently spark community pushback and forks.

The **Open Replenishment Framework (ORF)** resolves this dilemma by attaching commercial value capture **not to copyable code**, but to **non-copyable, stateful, and registry-governed assets**.

## 3. Mechanisms of ORF Fork Resistance

1. **Official Registry & Brand Badging ("Sustains-the-Commons")**:
   - While a third party can fork the code, they cannot fork the official ecosystem registry, certification mark, or compliance badge.
   - Commercial applications pay for canonical verification listed on the official ecosystem registry.

2. **Enterprise SLA Maintenance Commitments**:
   - Enterprise SLAs promise guaranteed security response times, patch backports, and designated maintainer availability.
   - A code fork does not copy the maintainers' time, expertise, or operational commitment.

3. **Canonical State & Liquidity Integration**:
   - In Web3 ecosystems, protocol fee splits ($\tau$) and yield sleeves flow from canonical state and liquidity networks.
   - Forking the codebase does not fork the underlying protocol liquidity or network effects.

---

## 4. Summary & Policy Takeaway

By anchoring value replenishment in non-copyable governance, maintenance SLA commitments, and canonical state networks, ORF achieves commercial replenishment **without restricting open-source code freedom**.
